\subsection{Sensitivity to the parameters $k$ and $t$}
The parameters $k$ and $t$ are chosen based on asymptotic considerations, but both papers leave the
specific constant values open. Fig.~\ref{fig:params} presents the running times on SF for all
combinations of $k\in\{1,\dots,6\}$ and $t\in\{2,\dots,10\}$, with the number of recursion levels
determined by $t$. Three observations hold for both SF and NA. First, setting $k=1$ consistently
produces poor performance. Without frontier reduction, every vertex in the frontier proceeds through
the recursion, making the algorithms significantly slower. On SF, they run up to
\ParSFTwoFiveWorst{} times slower than Dijkstra's algorithm for \bmA{} and up to \ParSFTwoSixWorst{}
times slower for \bmB{}. Second, the prescribed parameter values are effective, but they do not
deliver the best performance. For \bmA{}, increasing $k$ generally improves performance, which
aligns with the structure of these networks. Their average degree ranges from 2.0 to 2.6, indicating
that they consist largely of chains. With a larger $k$, additional relaxation rounds can process
longer chains within \fp. On SF, the fastest configuration is $k=\ParSFTwoFiveBestK$,
$t=\ParSFTwoFiveBestT$, which runs \ParSFTwoFiveGain\% faster than the prescribed setting. On NA,
the best configuration is $k=\ParNATwoFiveBestK$, $t=\ParNATwoFiveBestT$, achieving a
\ParNATwoFiveGain\% speedup. For \bmB{}, the fastest configurations use $t=\ParSFTwoSixBestT$ on
both networks, reducing the number of levels from four to two. These settings improve performance by
\ParSFTwoSixGain\% on SF and \ParNATwoSixGain\% on NA. Third, changing the parameter settings does
not alter the overall conclusion. Across the entire parameter grid, the best running time ratios
relative to Dijkstra's algorithm are \ParSFTwoFiveBest{} and \ParSFTwoSixBest{} on SF, and
\ParNATwoFiveBest{} and \ParNATwoSixBest{} on NA, for \bmA{} and \bmB{}, respectively.

The topmost recursion level in \bmB{} warrants further explanation. The call immediately below the
top level can process up to $t^32^{(L-1)t}$ vertices, where $L=\lceil\log n/t\rceil$. This capacity
is at least $n$ whenever $t^3\ge 2^t$, which holds for $t\le 9$. Consequently, for every $n$ up to
$2^{40}$ (Table~\ref{tab:params}), the top-level call extracts the source, receives the entire
vertex set $V$ from its single recursive subcall, and then relaxes every arc once more without
producing any further changes. This behavior is reflected in the operation counts: \bmB{} scans
every arc once in a base case and once at each recursion level.

\subsection{Scaling on synthetic families}
Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling} compares the running times of the algorithms relative to Dijkstra's algorithm
as the graph size $n$ increases from $2^{10}$ to $2^{\ScaleRandMaxExp}$ for random and grid graphs,
and up to $2^{\ScaleBaMaxExp}$ for preferential-attachment and hub graphs. On random directed
graphs, the relative running time decreases steadily as the graph size increases. For \bmA{}, the
ratio drops from \ScaleRandTwoFiveFirst{} at $n=2^{10}$ to \ScaleRandTwoFiveLast{} at $n=2^{22}$,
while \bmB{} improves from \ScaleRandTwoSixFirst{} to \ScaleRandTwoSixLast{} over the same range.
Preferential-attachment graphs show a similar pattern, with ratios of \ScaleBaTwoFiveLast{} for
\bmA{} and \ScaleBaTwoSixLast{} for \bmB{} at $n=2^{21}$. The heavy-tailed degree distribution of
these graphs does not produce any noticeable difference in performance. Grid graphs show a different
trend, as the improvement levels off at an earlier stage. Starting at $n=2^{14}$, both algorithms
remain between \GridLateMin{} and \GridLateMax{} times slower than Dijkstra's algorithm. On hub
graphs, \bmA{} improves to a ratio of \ScaleHubTwoFiveLast{}, whereas \bmB{} remains
\ScaleHubTwoSixLast{} times slower than Dijkstra at the largest tested graph size. Across all
\SynthPoints{} synthetic instances, \bmB{} is slower than \bmA{}, and neither of the two newer
algorithms outperforms Dijkstra's algorithm anywhere within the tested range. The performance curves
are not smooth because the prescribed values of $k$, $t$ and the number of recursion levels are
rounded and change in discrete steps. For graphs containing more than $2^{20}$ vertices, the two
Dijkstra variants also begin to diverge in performance. The \textsc{DecreaseKey} variant becomes up
to \DkLargeMax{} times slower, suggesting that, at these graph sizes, all algorithms are
increasingly affected by memory-hierarchy effects that are not captured by the cost model.

Fig.~\ref{fig:cmp} presents the same instances using the cost model described in the theorems,
revealing different trends across graph families. On random and preferential-attachment graphs, the
number of comparisons performed by Dijkstra's algorithm per arc increases linearly with $\log n$.
With the lazy heap, this count rises by \SlopeRandDij{} and \SlopeBaDij{} comparisons per arc for
each doubling of $n$, respectively. With the \textsc{DecreaseKey} variant, the corresponding
increases are \SlopeRandDijDK{} and \SlopeBaDijDK{}. In contrast, the comparison counts for the
BMSSP algorithms remain nearly constant. On random graphs, \bmA{} performs between
\CmpRangeRandTwoFiveMin{} and \CmpRangeRandTwoFiveMax{} comparisons per arc, while \bmB{} performs
between \CmpRangeRandTwoSixMin{} and \CmpRangeRandTwoSixMax{}. As a result, the curves eventually
cross. For random graphs, \bmA{} requires fewer comparisons than binary-heap Dijkstra at every
tested size from $n=2^{\CrossCmpRandTwoFiveDij}$ onward. For preferential-attachment graphs, this
advantage appears from $n=2^{\CrossCmpBaTwoFiveDij}$ onward. When compared with the
\textsc{DecreaseKey} variant, \bmA{} achieves the same advantage from
$n=2^{\CrossCmpRandTwoFiveDijDK}$ for random graphs and $n=2^{\CrossCmpBaTwoFiveDijDK}$ for
preferential-attachment graphs. Considering the combined number of comparisons and additions, \bmA{}
falls below Dijkstra's count from $n=2^{\CrossOpsRandTwoFiveDij}$ and $n=2^{\CrossOpsBaTwoFiveDij}$,
respectively. \bmB{} also eventually requires fewer comparisons than Dijkstra's algorithm, with its
comparison count dropping below Dijkstra's at $n=2^{\CrossCmpRandTwoSixDij}$ in both graph families.
These results show the asymptotic advantage of the new algorithms emerging at practical graph sizes
within the cost model. Although the theoretical advantage is established by the cost model, it does
not necessarily translate into faster execution. For example, at $n=2^{20}$ on random graphs, \bmA{}
performs \OpsRandTwentyTwoFive{} comparisons and additions per arc, compared with
\OpsRandTwentyDij{} for Dijkstra's algorithm, yet it still takes \TimeRandTwentyTwoFive{} times as
long to run. On grid and hub graphs, the comparison curves do not cross, for two different reasons
discussed below.

\subsection{Graph structure: frontier width and maximum degree}\label{sec:structure}
Two properties of the input graphs help explain the performance differences across the graph
families.

\emph{Frontier width.} We measure the width of Dijkstra's frontier by the maximum number of vertices
held in its indexed heap at any point during execution. On road networks, this value ranges from
\RoadPeakMin\% to \RoadPeakMax\% of $n$ (Table~\ref{tab:data}). On grid graphs, the frontier width
decreases from \PeakGridSmall\% at $n=2^{10}$ to \PeakGrid\% at $n=2^{22}$. Because the heap remains
small, extracting vertices is inexpensive, leaving little ordering work for a more sophisticated
algorithm to eliminate. Consequently, Dijkstra's algorithm performs fewer comparisons than both new
algorithms at every tested graph size. In contrast, on random and preferential-attachment graphs,
the frontier contains approximately \PeakRand\% and \PeakBa\% of the vertices, respectively, across
all tested sizes. Dijkstra's algorithm therefore incurs the full logarithmic factor associated with
maintaining the heap, allowing the comparison counts of the new algorithms to fall below its count.
The running time results follow a similar pattern: the lowest ratios relative to Dijkstra's
algorithm occur on random and preferential-attachment graphs, while higher ratios are observed on
grids and road networks.

\emph{Maximum degree.} On hub graphs, the frontier reaches its maximum possible width because all
$n$ vertices receive labels as soon as the source vertex is settled. Despite this, both BMSSP
algorithms perform more comparisons than Dijkstra's algorithm. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:cmp}
(right), \bmA{} requires \HubCmpRatioMinTwoFive{} to \HubCmpRatioMaxTwoFive{} times as many
comparisons, with the gap increasing as $n$ grows, while \bmB{} requires \HubCmpRatioMinTwoSix{} to
\HubCmpRatioMaxTwoSix{} times as many. The operation counters help explain this behavior. After the
first base case, all $n-1$ arcs of the hub are relaxed together. In \bmA{}, these arcs fall within
the range that is batch-prepended at level one. Since each block contains a single key, the
algorithm orders them through repeated median selection. In \bmB{}, the keys are inserted into the
base case's search tree and then passed upward through merge operations. In both cases, the
algorithm must order the entire frontier, and the cost per key is higher than that of a heap. The
theoretical analyses exclude this situation through their degree bound, which limits the number of
keys that a single completed vertex can generate. Table~\ref{tab:hub} confirms that the missing
degree bound is responsible for this behavior. After degree reduction with $\delta=3$, the instance
contains eight times as many vertices and satisfies the required precondition. On this transformed
graph, \bmB{} performs fewer than half as many comparisons as Dijkstra's algorithm:
\HubRedCmpTwoSix{} compared with \HubRedCmpDij{} per arc of the original graph. \bmA{} also performs
fewer comparisons, requiring \HubRedCmpTwoFive{} per arc of the original graph. However, this
advantage comes at a substantial computational cost. Dijkstra's algorithm on the original graph
requires only \HubCmpDij{} comparisons per arc, while the degree-reduction transformation alone
makes it \HubRedDijSlow{} times slower. Even on the transformed graph, \bmA{} and \bmB{} take
\HubRedSameTwoFive{} and \HubRedSameTwoSix{} times as long, respectively, as Dijkstra's algorithm
running on that same transformed graph.

\subsection{Web, road and social graphs}\label{sec:snap}
Table~\ref{tab:snap} and Fig.~\ref{fig:snap} extend the evaluation to \SnapGraphs{} larger
real world graphs. Across all \SnapInstances{} instances, Dijkstra's algorithm delivers the fastest
running time. \bmA{} runs between \SnapRatioMinTwoFive{} and \SnapRatioMaxTwoFive{} times slower
than Dijkstra's algorithm, whereas \bmB{} is between \SnapRatioMinTwoSix{} and \SnapRatioMaxTwoSix{}
times slower, and \bmB{} outperforms \bmA{} on \SnapTwoSixFaster{} of the tested instances. Among
these results, the closest performance to Dijkstra's algorithm is observed on \SnapBestInstance, the
densest graph having the widest frontier.
In case of having unit weights, the setup in which~\cite{ryu2025} reports its simplified solver
ahead of Dijkstra, the new algorithms are \SnapUnitTimeMinTwoFive{} to \SnapUnitTimeMaxTwoFive{}
(\bmA) and \SnapUnitTimeMinTwoSix{} to \SnapUnitTimeMaxTwoSix{} (\bmB) times slower; we observed
no practical advantage on any of the graphs that we chose.

The counts are similar to the pattern of Section~\ref{sec:structure}. The frontiers of the three
road networks are at most \SnapRoadPeakMax\% of $n$, and both the published algorithms require
more comparisons than Dijkstra's algorithm. The web and social graphs having frontiers between
\SnapOtherPeakMin\% and \SnapOtherPeakMax\% of $n$; with random weights \bmA{} need fewer
comparisons than the lazy-heap baseline on \SnapFewerCmpTwoFive{} of them (\SnapFewerListTwoFive),
the two of the largest. Two qualifications apply. Measured against the \textsc{DecreaseKey}
variant, whose heap never exceeds $n$ entries therefore is much cheaper on graphs which are dense,
\bmA{} needs more comparisons on all of the \SnapInstances{} instances. And with unit weights,
where Dijkstra's algorithm needs few comparisons per arc, the new algorithms need more of them on
every graph (\SnapUnitCmpMinTwoFive{} to \SnapUnitCmpMaxTwoFive{} times for \bmA).

\subsection{Density and weight range}
Two sweeps on random digraphs with $n=2^{18}$ vary further properties. On increasing the average
out-degree from 2 to 8, the ratio to Dijkstra from \DensLowTwoFive{} to \DensMinTwoFive{} for
\bmA{} and from \DensLowTwoSix{} to \DensMinTwoSix{} for \bmB{} lowers; beyond that the ratio
remains roughly constant. Graphs which are denser spend a larger share of the time in plain edge
scans, which is performed by all algorithms, but the BMSSP algorithms scan each arc three to four
times, because of which the ratio does not approach one. The weight range has little influence:
from unit weights, where all paths with the same number of arcs tie and the tie-breaking rule
decides many comparisons, to weights up to $2^{30}$ the ratio stays between \WeightMinTwoFive{}
and \WeightMaxTwoFive{} for \bmA{} and between \WeightMinTwoSix{} and \WeightMaxTwoSix{} for \bmB{}.

\subsection{Memory}
The BMSSP implementations keeps a one direct-address table for each recursion level in addition to
the labels. On the random graph with $n=2^{22}$ the peak resident set of the whole process, which
includes the graph and the reference solution, is \MemDij~MB for Dijkstra, \MemTwoFive~MB for
\bmA{} and \MemTwoSix~MB for \bmB, a factor of \MemRatioTwoFive{} and \MemRatioTwoSix.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{What \bmB{} gains over \bmA?} The redesigned \fp{} does what is promised. On the road
networks it accounts for \PivotScanMinTwoSix{} to \PivotScanMaxTwoSix{} edge scans per arc,
against \PivotScanMinTwoFive{} to \PivotScanMaxTwoFive{} for the relaxation rounds of \bmA. The
savings are lost by the side effects of the new parameter setting. The value of $t$ is smaller
than in \bmA{} (Table~\ref{tab:params}), which gives more levels, and every level relaxes all arcs
once: the relaxation loops cost \MainScanMinTwoSix{} to \MainScanMaxTwoSix{} scans per arc against
\MainScanMinTwoFive{} to \MainScanMaxTwoFive. In addition the base case is now a search-tree
Dijkstra over up to $t^3+1$ vertices instead of a heap with a small number of entries. In total
\bmB{} performs more additions than \bmA{} on \RoadsMoreAddsTwoSix{} of the five road networks and
more comparisons on \RoadsMoreCmpTwoSix{} of them, and it was slower on \RealTwoSixSlowerWord{} of
the five road networks and on all synthetic instances. When $t$ is chosen freely the fastest
configurations of \bmB{} have two levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:params}), which suggests that the balance
between $t$ and the cost of \fp{} that is optimal asymptotically is not the practical at these
sizes.

\emph{Where the time goes.} For the narrow-frontier inputs the new algorithms lose twice: they
execute \OpsRatioMinTwoFive{} to \OpsRatioMaxTwoFive{} (\bmA) and \OpsRatioMinTwoSix{} to
\OpsRatioMaxTwoSix{} (\bmB) times as many comparisons and additions as Dijkstra's algorithm on
the road networks, and each one of these counted operations costs them \PerOpMinTwoFive{} to
\PerOpMaxTwoFive{} and \PerOpMinTwoSix{} to \PerOpMaxTwoSix{} times as much time. For
wide-frontier inputs of bounded degree the first handicap disappears as $n$ grows, but the second
remains and keeps the new algorithms behind. It is tied to features of the design that the cost
model does not penalize: every arc is scanned three to five times instead of just once, labels
are four-field tuples instead of just single numbers, and the block structures add indirection
and bookkeeping. Reducing the number of passes over the arcs, for instance by skipping the
redundant topmost level of \bmB, attacks the right term, but our parameter sweep indicates gains
of tens of percent and not the factors.

\emph{Relation to earlier measurements.} Our conclusion agrees with every study of an
implementation that follows the published algorithm: \cite{castro2025} report Dijkstra to be
three to four times faster on large instances, \cite{valko2025} find it faster on Lightning
Network graphs, and the solver of~\cite{ryu2025} that follows the paper is reported as two to
four times slower. Since our \bmA{} has the same structure as the worst case implementation
of~\cite{castro2025}, which uses the same parameters, per-level tables and deletion repair, its
published results also provide a useful check that our implementation is not unusually slow. For
random graphs with $m=3n$, the authors report running times of 10.7~ms for Dijkstra's algorithm and
38.2~ms for BMSSP at $n=2^{16}$, 421~ms and 1286~ms at $n=2^{20}$, and 2023~ms and 7703~ms at
$n=2^{22}$, respectively, using an Intel i5-10400F. On an Apple M1, we measure \MsSixteenDij~ms and
\MsSixteenTwoFive~ms, \MsTwentyDij~ms and \MsTwentyTwoFive~ms, and \MsTwentyTwoDij~ms and
\MsTwentyTwoTwoFive~ms for the same graph sizes. Both of our implementations run faster in absolute
terms, with the BMSSP implementation showing the larger improvement. As a result, our running time
ratios of \RatioRandSixteenTwoFive, \RatioRandTwentyTwoFive{} and \RatioRandTwentyTwoTwoFive{} are
lower than the corresponding ratios reported in~\cite{castro2025}, which are 3.6, 3.1 and 3.8.

The two sets of results also differ in how the ratios change with graph size. In~\cite{castro2025},
the ratio decreases from 25 at $n=2^{7}$ to approximately three at $n=2^{14}$, after which it
remains between 2.8 and 3.8 up to $n=2^{25}$. In our experiments, the ratio continues to decline
gradually up to $n=2^{22}$. However, the experiments use different machines and graph generators,
and we did not run the implementation from~\cite{castro2025}. Therefore, we cannot determine whether
these differences arise from the implementations or the hardware, and the comparison should be
treated as indicative rather than conclusive. The practical advantages over Dijkstra's algorithm
reported in the studies we are aware of,~\cite{ryu2025} and~\cite{webb2025}, concern simplified
variants operating on unit-weight graphs (Section~II). In contrast, Section~\ref{sec:snap} shows
that the published algorithms do not achieve such an advantage on the same graphs.

\emph{Threats to validity.} All runtime measurements were collected on a single machine using a
single compiler. The implementations follow the pseudocode and incorporate sensible engineering
choices, including flat arrays, direct-address tables, and avoiding per-call memory allocation.
However, they have not been optimized to their full potential. In addition, the base case of \bmB{}
uses a node-based search tree because this is what its pseudocode specifies. The operation counts
are only partly independent of these implementation choices: they also depend on the heap and
selection routines being used. For example, a heap that requires fewer comparisons per operation
would shift Dijkstra's curves downward. All edge weights are integers, and the effects of rounding
when using floating point weights have not been investigated. The largest instances contain $2^{22}$
vertices, which is still far from the scale at which the asymptotic bounds begin to separate.
Therefore, we make no claims about the instance size at which a crossover in running time might
occur. Finally, the two observations discussed in Section~\ref{sec:correct} are based on our
interpretation of the pseudocode in~\cite{duan2026} and have not been verified by the authors of
that work.
